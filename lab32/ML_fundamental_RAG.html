    • 1. Machine Learning vs Deep Learning

    Machine Learning (ML) and Deep Learning (DL) are related, but deep learning is a subset of machine learning.

    1.1 Comparison

    Feature Machine Learning Deep Learning
    DefinitionAI methods that learn patterns from dataML methods based mainly on multi-layer neural networks
    Data needsCan work well with small to medium datasetsUsually benefits from very large datasets
    Feature extractionOften designed manually by humansOften learned automatically by the network
    ComputeUsually less demandingUsually requires much more compute
    Training timeOften fasterOften slower
    InterpretabilityOften easier to understandOften harder to interpret
    Best forStructured or tabular data, forecasting, classificationImages, speech, video, language, and complex patterns
    ExamplesLinear regression, decision trees, SVM, random forestsCNNs, RNNs, Transformers

    1.2 Simple Example

    Suppose you want a computer to recognize a cat in a picture.

    Traditional Machine Learning:
    A human might first define useful features such as ear shape, fur texture, eye shape, and whiskers. Then an ML algorithm uses those features to classify the image.
    Deep Learning:
    You can give the neural network many labeled images. The network learns useful features by itself, progressing from simple patterns such as edges to more complex patterns such as eyes, ears, and eventually whole objects.

    1.3 Relationship

    Artificial Intelligence → Machine Learning → Deep Learning

    Therefore, every deep-learning system is a machine-learning system, but not every machine-learning system uses deep learning.

    1.4 Rule of Thumb

    Traditional machine learning is often very effective for structured data, while deep learning becomes especially powerful for complex unstructured data such as images, audio, and natural language.

    2. CNN vs RNN vs Transformers

    CNNs, RNNs, and Transformers are all types of neural-network architectures, but they are designed to process information in different ways.

    2.1 Quick Comparison

    Feature CNN RNN Transformer
    Full name Convolutional Neural Network Recurrent Neural Network Transformer
    Main idea Look for local patterns Process data sequentially Use attention to relate relevant parts
    Traditionally best for Images Sequential and time-series data Language, multimodal data, and long sequences
    Processing Local regions One step after another Many tokens can be processed in parallel during training
    Memory of earlier input Limited by receptive field and layers Hidden state carries prior information Attention accesses other tokens directly
    Long-range relationships Moderate Often difficult Strong
    Parallel training Strong Limited Strong
    Examples ResNet, U-Net LSTM, GRU BERT, GPT, Llama

    2.2 CNN — Convolutional Neural Network

    A CNN is especially good at detecting local spatial patterns.

    Imagine an image of a cat. A CNN does not initially understand “cat.” Its early layers may detect:

    edges → curves → shapes → eyes/ears → face → cat

    A small filter, called a kernel, moves across an image and calculates features.

    Simplified convolution:
    yi,j = Σm Σn Xi+m,j+nKm,n

    The filter may become sensitive to features such as vertical edges.

    Intuition: “Look at small neighborhoods and find useful patterns.”

    CNNs are widely used for image classification, object detection, medical imaging, and image segmentation.

    2.3 RNN — Recurrent Neural Network

    An RNN was designed for sequences, such as:

    I → love → learning → AI

    Instead of treating every word independently, an RNN maintains a hidden state containing information from previous steps.

    Simplified equation:
    ht = f(Wxxt + Whht-1 + b)

    where xt is the current input, ht-1 is the previous memory, ht is the new memory, and Wx and Wh are learned weights.

    Information therefore flows sequentially:

    Word 1 → Word 2 → Word 3 → Word 4

    Basic RNNs often struggle with long-distance relationships because information must pass through many sequential steps. LSTM and GRU networks were developed to improve this problem.

    Intuition: “Read one thing at a time and carry a memory forward.”

    2.4 Transformer

    The Transformer introduced a major change. Instead of processing words mainly one at a time, it uses attention so that each token can directly examine other relevant tokens.

    Example:
    “The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired.”

    To understand “it,” the model should connect it strongly with “animal.”

    Attention allows the model to learn that relationship directly.

    Core attention calculation:
    Attention(Q,K,V) = softmax(QKT / √dk)V
    • Q = Query: What am I looking for?
    • K = Key: What information does each token contain?
    • V = Value: What information should be passed forward?
    Input tokens → Query/Key/Value → Attention scores → Relevant relationships → Weighted information → Next layer

    This architecture is the foundation of modern systems such as GPT, BERT, Llama, and many multimodal models.

    Intuition: “Look at the whole relevant context and decide what deserves attention.”

    2.5 The Key Difference

    Consider the sentence:

    “The dog that was playing with the children in the park was hungry.”

    A CNN primarily examines nearby patterns.

    An RNN processes the sentence sequentially:

    The → dog → that → was → playing → ... → hungry

    Information about “dog” must survive through the sequence.

    A Transformer can directly establish:

    dog ↔ hungry

    through attention, even though the two words are separated. This ability is one major reason Transformers became so successful for language.

    2.6 Easy Analogy

    Imagine three students trying to understand a long book:

    CNN: Reads small groups of nearby words and looks for patterns.
    RNN: Reads the book from beginning to end while trying to remember what happened earlier.
    Transformer: Can look across the relevant text and ask, “Which earlier parts are most important for understanding what I am reading now?”

    2.7 Relationship to Deep Learning

    All three are deep-learning architectures:

    AI → Machine Learning → Deep Learning → CNN / RNN / Transformer

    They are not three different levels of AI. They are different architectural approaches within deep learning.

    A particularly important historical progression for language applications is:

    RNN → LSTM/GRU → Transformer → modern LLMs

    CNNs remain extremely useful, particularly in computer vision, even though Transformers are now also heavily used for vision and multimodal AI.

    3. RAG Generation: RAG-Sequence vs RAG-Token

    This is describing the original RAG formulation from Lewis et al. (2020). The key idea is that RAG combines two probability models:

    Retriever pη(z|x)     +     Generator pθ(yi|x,z,y1:i−1)

    The retriever decides which passages are useful, while the generator decides which output tokens should be produced given the query and a retrieved passage. Lewis et al. implemented the generator using a pretrained sequence-to-sequence model, BART-large, and proposed two ways to combine retrieved passages with generation: RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token.

    3.1 First understand the notation

    Suppose the user asks:

    x: “Who wrote The Old Man and the Sea?”

    The retriever searches a document collection and returns passages such as:

    z1, z2, z3, …, zk

    For example:

    z1 = “The Old Man and the Sea is a novel by Ernest Hemingway.”

    z2 = “Hemingway received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1954.”

    z3 = “The novel was published in 1952.”

    The desired answer might be:

    y = (y1, y2) = (Ernest, Hemingway)

    The symbols mean:

    SymbolMeaning
    xinput query
    zretrieved passage/document
    ycomplete generated answer
    yitoken generated at position i
    y1:i−1all previously generated tokens
    pη(z|x)retriever probability
    pθ(yi|x,z,y1:i−1)generator probability
    ηretriever parameters
    θgenerator parameters
    knumber of retrieved passages
    Nnumber of tokens in the output

    3.2 The generator probability

    The expression

    pθ(yi|x,z,y1:i−1)

    is extremely important.

    It says:

    Given the query x, the retrieved passage z, and the tokens already generated y1,…,yi−1, what is the probability that the next token is yi?

    For example, suppose we have:

    x = “Who wrote The Old Man and the Sea?”

    z = “The Old Man and the Sea was written by Ernest Hemingway.”

    At the first output position, the model evaluates something like:

    pθ(y1|x,z)

    Maybe its vocabulary probabilities are:

    P(Ernest)=0.70
    P(William)=0.05
    P(The)=0.03

    and so on.

    Suppose it selects:

    y1 = Ernest

    At the next step:

    pθ(y2|x,z,Ernest)

    might give:

    P(Hemingway)=0.90
    P(Smith)=0.02
    P(Johnson)=0.01

    Thus:

    y2 = Hemingway

    The answer becomes:

    y = (Ernest, Hemingway)

    This is ordinary autoregressive generation.

    3.3 Probability of an entire output sequence

    Because each token depends on previous tokens, the probability of the complete sequence is obtained using the probability chain rule:

    pθ(y|x,z) = ∏i=1N pθ(yi|x,z,y1:i−1)

    For our two-token answer:

    y = (Ernest, Hemingway)

    suppose:

    P(Ernest|x,z)=0.70
    P(Hemingway|x,z,Ernest)=0.90

    Then:

    P(y|x,z) = 0.70 × 0.90

    so:

    P(y|x,z)=0.63

    The complete answer therefore receives probability 0.63 under that particular passage.

    3.4 But RAG retrieves multiple passages

    RAG does not normally depend on only one passage.

    The retriever computes:

    pη(z|x)

    for possible documents.

    Suppose the top three retrieved passages have:

    pη(z1|x)=0.60
    pη(z2|x)=0.30
    pη(z3|x)=0.10

    These represent the retriever's belief about how relevant each passage is.

    Conceptually:

    x → z1 (0.60)
    x → z2 (0.30)
    x → z3 (0.10)

    Now RAG must combine these retrieval probabilities with generator probabilities.

    That is where RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token differ.

    3.5 RAG-Sequence

    The RAG-Sequence equation is:

    pRAG-Sequence(y|x) ≈ Σz∈top-k pη(z|x) ∏i=1N pθ(yi|x,z,y1:i−1)

    There are two major operations:

    Σz     and     ∏i

    Notice their order:

    Σz [ ∏i ]

    That ordering captures the central idea of RAG-Sequence.

    3.5.1 Intuition

    For each passage z:

    1. Keep that same passage.
    2. Generate/evaluate the whole answer using it.
    3. Calculate the probability of that whole sequence.
    4. Weight the result by the passage's retrieval probability.
    5. Combine the possibilities across retrieved passages.

    Lewis et al. describe this formulation as using the same latent document for all target tokens in a generated sequence.

    Conceptually:

    z1 → y1,y2,y3,…,yN

    or

    z2 → y1,y2,y3,…,yN

    or

    z3 → y1,y2,y3,…,yN

    Each possible answer sequence is evaluated under a particular passage.

    3.6 Mathematical example of RAG-Sequence

    Suppose we retrieve three passages:

    P(z1|x)=0.6
    P(z2|x)=0.3
    P(z3|x)=0.1

    We want to evaluate the two-token answer:

    y = (Ernest, Hemingway)

    Assume the generator probabilities are:

    Passage P(Ernest) P(Hemingway | Ernest)
    z10.800.90
    z20.500.60
    z30.200.30

    First calculate the sequence probability for each passage.

    For z1:

    P(y|x,z1) = 0.80 × 0.90 = 0.72

    For z2:

    P(y|x,z2) = 0.50 × 0.60 = 0.30

    For z3:

    P(y|x,z3) = 0.20 × 0.30 = 0.06

    Now multiply each by its retrieval probability:

    z1: 0.6 × 0.72 = 0.432
    z2: 0.3 × 0.30 = 0.090
    z3: 0.1 × 0.06 = 0.006

    Finally sum:

    PRAG-Seq(y|x) = 0.432 + 0.090 + 0.006

    Therefore:

    PRAG-Seq(y|x)=0.528

    3.7 What is being marginalized?

    The summation:

    Σz

    is an example of marginalization.

    The model does not know which passage is the single “true” passage responsible for the answer.

    So z is treated as a latent variable.

    Instead of saying:

    z = z1

    with certainty, the model considers several possibilities:

    P(y|x) = Σz P(z|x)P(y|x,z)

    This is simply the law of total probability.

    The RAG equation expands:

    P(y|x,z)

    into its autoregressive token probabilities:

    P(y|x,z) = ∏i P(yi|x,z,y<i)

    giving:

    P(y|x) = Σz P(z|x) ∏i P(yi|x,z,y<i)

    which is exactly the RAG-Sequence formulation.

    3.8 RAG-Token

    RAG-Token changes where the summation over documents occurs:

    pRAG-Token(y|x) ≈ ∏i=1N Σz∈top-k pη(z|x) pθ(yi|x,z,y1:i−1)

    Notice the order now:

    ∏i [ Σz ]

    Compare that with RAG-Sequence:

    Σz [ ∏i ]

    This small-looking mathematical change has an important interpretation.

    3.9 RAG-Token chooses evidence at every token

    For every token yi, RAG-Token considers all retrieved passages again.

    For token 1:

    P(y1|x) = Σz P(z|x) P(y1|x,z)

    Then token 2:

    P(y2|x,y1) = Σz P(z|x) P(y2|x,z,y1)

    Then token 3:

    P(y3|x,y1,y2) = Σz P(z|x) P(y3|x,z,y1,y2)

    and so forth.

    Therefore different passages can contribute strongly to different tokens. This is precisely the distinction Lewis et al. made between their two formulations: RAG-Token permits different retrieved passages to contribute at different output positions, whereas RAG-Sequence marginalizes a single passage across the entire sequence.

    3.10 Mathematical example of RAG-Token

    Use the same retrieval probabilities:

    P(z1|x)=0.6
    P(z2|x)=0.3
    P(z3|x)=0.1

    Again generate:

    y = (Ernest, Hemingway)

    For the first token, suppose:

    PassageP(Ernest | x,z)
    z10.80
    z20.50
    z30.20

    RAG-Token combines them:

    P(Ernest) = 0.6(0.80) + 0.3(0.50) + 0.1(0.20)
    =0.48+0.15+0.02
    P(Ernest)=0.65

    Now for the second token suppose:

    PassageP(Hemingway | Ernest)
    z10.90
    z20.60
    z30.30

    Then:

    P(Hemingway) = 0.6(0.90) + 0.3(0.60) + 0.1(0.30)
    =0.54+0.18+0.03
    =0.75

    The entire answer probability is:

    P(y|x)=0.65×0.75

    so:

    PRAG-Token(y|x)=0.4875

    Notice that this is not the same as RAG-Sequence's 0.528.

    That is because:

    Σz∏i(·) ≠ ∏iΣz(·)

    in general.

    That difference is the mathematical heart of the two RAG models.

    3.11 A better example showing why RAG-Token can mix passages

    Suppose the question is:

    “Where was Einstein born, and what year was he born?”

    Imagine two retrieved passages.

    Passage z1

    Albert Einstein was born in Ulm, Germany.

    Passage z2

    Albert Einstein was born on March 14, 1879.

    Suppose the desired answer is:

    “Ulm in 1879.”

    For the token Ulm, passage z1 might be very strong:

    P(Ulm|z1)=0.95

    but:

    P(Ulm|z2)=0.15

    For 1879, however:

    P(1879|z1)=0.20

    and:

    P(1879|z2)=0.96

    RAG-Token naturally allows:

    z1 → Ulm
    z2 → 1879

    Conceptually:

    z1 → y1     z2 → y2

    RAG-Sequence instead scores the whole output under each passage before combining the passage-level sequence probabilities.

    3.12 Why the generator is called pθ

    The subscript:

    θ

    represents the generator's learned parameters.

    In a Transformer such as BART, θ consists of enormous collections of learned values:

    θ = {WQ,WK,WV,WO, WFFN, embeddings, biases,…}

    These parameters determine the probability distribution:

    pθ(yi|x,z,y<i)

    In the original RAG experiments, the parametric generator was based on BART-large, while the retrieval side used a dense passage retrieval setup over Wikipedia. BART itself is a Transformer encoder-decoder pretrained as a denoising sequence-to-sequence model.

    3.13 What goes into BART?

    For each retrieved document, the generator receives something conceptually like:

    [query ; passage]

    For example:

    Who wrote The Old Man and the Sea?
    The Old Man and the Sea is a 1952 novel written by Ernest Hemingway...

    The BART encoder transforms this input into contextual representations:

    H = Encoderθ(x,z)

    The decoder then autoregressively produces:

    y1,y2,…,yN

    At step i:

    hidecoder = Decoderθ(y<i,H)

    The decoder's hidden vector is projected to vocabulary logits:

    ℓi = Wohi + b

    Then softmax converts those logits into probabilities:

    P(yi=v) = eℓi,v / Σu∈Veℓi,u

    where V is the vocabulary.

    That probability is precisely the quantity represented abstractly by:

    pθ(yi|x,z,y<i)

    3.14 Putting retriever and generator together

    The overall system can therefore be viewed as:

    x
    ↓
    Retriever
    pη(z|x)
    ↓
    z1,z2,…,zk
    ↓
    Generator
    For every passage:
    [x;zj] → BART Encoder → BART Decoder
    ↓
    pθ(yi|x,zj,y<i)

    Then RAG combines the probabilities using either:

    RAG-Sequence

    Σz pη(z|x) ∏i pθ(yi|x,z,y<i)

    or:

    RAG-Token

    ∏i Σz pη(z|x) pθ(yi|x,z,y<i)

    3.15 The most important mathematical difference

    Put the two next to each other:

    RAG-Sequence

    Σz [ pη(z|x) ∏i pθ(yi|x,z,y<i) ]

    one passage explains the entire sequence

    RAG-Token

    ∏i [ Σz pη(z|x) pθ(yi|x,z,y<i) ]

    passages are mixed for each token

    So an intuitive memory trick is:

    RAG-Sequence: choose/marginalize evidence at the answer level.

    RAG-Token: choose/marginalize evidence at the token level.

    3.16 One correction to the wording in your notes

    This sentence:

    “RAG-Token ... ranks individually generated tokens rather than the entire sequence”

    is useful intuitively, but it is not mathematically the most precise description.

    More precisely, RAG-Token marginalizes over the retrieved documents separately for each output token:

    Σz pη(z|x) pθ(yi|x,z,y<i)

    It is not simply ranking tokens independently. The token is still conditioned on:

    y1:i−1

    so generation remains autoregressive.

    Likewise, RAG-Sequence does not merely create k answers and pick the highest one in the mathematical definition. Its probability model marginalizes over the retrieved documents, although decoding procedures can approximate the search over candidate sequences. The original paper discusses distinct decoding strategies for RAG-Sequence.

    3.17 Compact summary

    The entire concept can be reduced to:

    RAG = Retrieval probability × Generation probability

    The retriever provides:

    pη(z|x)

    “How relevant is passage z to query x?”

    The generator provides:

    pθ(yi|x,z,y<i)

    “Given the query, this passage, and what I have written so far, how likely is the next token?”

    Then:

    RAG-Sequence = Σz [ passage probability × whole-answer probability ]

    whereas:

    RAG-Token = ∏i [ Σz passage probability × next-token probability ]

    The most important distinction is therefore:

    RAG-Sequence: Σz∏i     vs.     RAG-Token: ∏iΣz

    That reversal of sum and product is what allows RAG-Token to combine evidence from different passages during different token-generation steps, while RAG-Sequence treats the retrieved passage as a sequence-level latent variable.

    3.18 RAG-Token vs RAG-Sequence Quick Comparison

    RAG-Sequence and RAG-Token differ in when they combine information from retrieved passages.

    Feature RAG-Sequence RAG-Token
    Passage choice One passage is treated as supporting the whole output sequence Different passages can influence different output tokens
    Mathematical form Σz pη(z|x) ∏i pθ(yi|x,z,y<i) ∏i Σz pη(z|x) pθ(yi|x,z,y<i)
    Main idea Combine passage evidence after scoring the whole answer Combine passage evidence at every token-generation step
    Evidence mixing Sequence level Token level
    Flexibility Lower Higher
    Intuition “Which passage best explains this whole answer?” “Which passage is most helpful for this next token?”

    The easiest way to remember the difference is:

    RAG-Sequence: Σz ∏i

    versus

    RAG-Token: ∏i Σz

    For example, suppose the question is:

    “Where was Einstein born, and in what year?”

    One retrieved passage says Ulm, Germany, while another says 1879.

    With RAG-Sequence, the model evaluates the whole answer under each retrieved passage and then combines those sequence-level probabilities.

    With RAG-Token, the token “Ulm” may be influenced mostly by the first passage, while “1879” may be influenced mostly by the second passage.

    RAG-Sequence = one passage acts as the latent evidence for the entire answer.

    RAG-Token = passage evidence is mixed again at each token position.
